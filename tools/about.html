We kept sharing links. Not everyone read them. So we made {{LOGO_SVG_INLINE}}

In the lab we passed around links: to events, to news, to things worth knowing about. Some of us read them. Not everyone did. We wanted a more tangible way to meet the week’s news.

So the news now has a body. Cyborg News is a two-page newspaper, set every week from the week’s reporting and made to be printed on A3. You can hold it, fold it, and hand it to the person next to you.

Nobody sets the type by hand. A script gathers the stories, a language model drafts them, and ordinary code fits them to the page, checks them, and prints them.

What’s in it?

A lead story with a photograph, a handful of others, a column of one-line briefs, and standing boxes for tables and notes. Every issue has a date on it and a place in the archive, so last month’s paper is still there.

It follows the lab’s own questions: how people and AI shape each other, what using AI does to us psychologically, AI and wellbeing, wearables and embodied computing, and a small beat for the industry. News from the Media Lab counts like any other source.

How does it work?

Every Sunday evening, Boston time, one run takes the paper from nothing to a finished PDF. A language model is asked specific questions at set points: what to cover, what to write. Ordinary code does everything else, and it has the last word.

  1. Collect

    Look back over the week

    Reads a fixed set of news, research and policy feeds for the past seven days, and sorts every item by theme so that no single source can fill the paper.

  2. Read

    Open every link

    Fetches each page and pulls out the article text, or the abstract when it is a paper. A link it cannot read is recorded as a failure and never guessed at.

  3. Choose

    Play editor

    A model picks the stories, the lead, and which ones earn a photograph, knowing exactly how many words each column holds.

  4. Write

    Write each story on its own

    Each article is written from its own sources only, to the length of its column, with the most important facts first so that the end can be cut.

  5. Illustrate

    Find a photograph that is allowed

    Searches Wikimedia Commons for a picture under a Creative Commons or public-domain licence, and keeps the licence on record for the credit line.

  6. Set

    Fit it to the page

    Typesets both pages, measures where a column ran short or long, and asks for a rewrite until the type fills the space.

  7. Check

    Try to break it

    Before anything is printed, code that has no opinions looks for three kinds of failure.

    Quotations. Every quotation has to appear word for word in the source it came from.
    Photographs. Every picture needs a recorded licence that permits reprinting.
    The lab. Nothing may claim anything about our lab. The paper knows nothing about it, so it says nothing.
    If it passesIt is printed as an A3 PDF and filed in the archive.
    ThenThe reader on this site updates, and the paper is there for Monday.
    If it failsNothing is published. Last week’s paper stays up and an issue is opened, so a person looks.
  8. Print

    Put it on paper

    Renders the A3 PDF, files it with its sources and plan, and publishes it here. Print it, fold it, pass it on.

The checks catch invented quotations, unlicensed pictures and claims about the lab. They cannot make every sentence true. The paper summarises other people’s reporting; it does no reporting of its own.

When does it come out?

On Sunday evening the run looks back over the previous seven days and sets the paper, so that it is there when Monday starts.

Why paper?

A link is easy to skip. A page on a table is not, and it travels: it gets folded into a bag, left on a desk, handed over with “have you seen this?”. The newspaper is a way of making the week’s news something you can pick up.

Who made it?

Cyborg News is built and looked after by one researcher in the lab. The code is open, so you can see exactly what it does and what it checks.
